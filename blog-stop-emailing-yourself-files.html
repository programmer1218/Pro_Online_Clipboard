Stop Emailing Yourself Files: 7 Faster Alternatives

July 2026

At some point, most of us developed the habit of emailing ourselves a link, a document, or a photo just to get it from one device to another. It works, technically. But it is also one of the slowest, most roundabout ways to move information between your own devices — and most people keep doing it simply because they have never stopped to look for something better. Here are seven alternatives worth trying instead.

1. A Browser-Based Clipboard Tool

Instead of opening a mail app, addressing an email to yourself, and waiting for it to arrive, a tool like Online Clipboard lets you paste text or upload a file, get a short code instantly, and type that code into any other device's browser to retrieve it. There is no inbox to check, no attachment to download separately, and no account required on either end. For quick, one-off transfers, this is usually the fastest option available.

2. Your Phone's Built-In Sharing (AirDrop / Nearby Share)

If you are moving something between two devices of the same brand ecosystem — two iPhones, or an iPhone and a Mac, or two Android devices — the built-in sharing feature is extremely fast because it works over local Bluetooth or Wi-Fi Direct rather than the internet. The only requirement is that both devices support the same protocol, which rules out mixing Apple and Android hardware.

3. A Synced Notes App

If you frequently need a "scratchpad" that follows you across devices, a notes app that syncs automatically (rather than one tied to a single device) removes the need to transfer things one at a time. This works well for ongoing lists and drafts, though it is less convenient for a single one-time transfer of a file or image.

4. Cloud Storage — But Only for Files You Will Reuse

Google Drive, Dropbox, and OneDrive are the right home for files you expect to open again later from multiple devices. The mistake is using them for one-time transfers, which clutters your storage with files you will never open again. Reserve cloud storage for things with lasting value, and use a faster method for everything transient.

5. QR Codes for Phone Retrieval

When you are sending something from a desktop to a phone, typing a URL or searching for a downloaded file is often more annoying than it needs to be. Many transfer tools, including Online Clipboard, generate a QR code alongside every transfer — pointing your phone's camera at the screen retrieves the content in one motion, no typing required.

6. A Password Manager's Built-In Sharing

If what you are actually trying to move is a password or login credential, resist the temptation to email or message it — both leave a permanent, searchable copy sitting in your inbox or chat history indefinitely. A password manager with cross-device sync, or a short-lived transfer tool that deletes itself after a day, is a meaningfully safer choice.

7. Direct Cable or Local Network Transfer

For very large files — video, full-resolution photo libraries, project backups — no internet-based method beats a direct USB connection or a local network transfer tool. It avoids upload and download time entirely and is not subject to any file size caps that free online tools impose to stay fast and sustainable.

The Common Thread

Every one of these alternatives shares one advantage over emailing yourself: fewer steps between having something and actually using it. Email was designed for asynchronous conversations with other people, not as a personal file-transfer pipe, and it shows every time you have to search your own inbox for "that link I sent myself last Tuesday." Once you build the habit of reaching for a faster, purpose-built option, going back to emailing yourself starts to feel like using a fax machine.
